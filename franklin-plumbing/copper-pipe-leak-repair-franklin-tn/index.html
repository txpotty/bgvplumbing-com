    Frequently Asked Questions About Copper Pipe Leak Repair in Franklin

    A minor, accessible pinhole repair starts around $200. Slab leaks run higher. Complex repairs like under-slab leaks can run past $2,000, with location, severity and footage of new pipe setting most of the price. You get a written number after locating.

    A visible pinhole or a small section is usually done in two to four hours. Hidden leaks add time. Slab leaks or a repipe can take a full day or more, with the method and access setting the pace. You get a timeline first.

    The pipe wall wears through from the inside. Water chemistry drives it. Aggressive water, high velocity, excess pressure, poor original soldering and stray electrical current from grounding issues each thin the wall until it pins through. Hot lines get it worst.

    Water chemistry is a big factor. Private wells serve many rural homes in Tennessee, and some well water carries iron, sulfur or sediment that calls for filtration or treatment. Pressure matters too. Water that is too acidic or too alkaline, aggressive soil around buried pipe, high pressure, poor installation and foundation movement each wear copper down or pull fittings apart. It is usually a combination.

    Insurance often covers sudden water damage. Worn pipe is usually excluded. The cost of repairing old or corroded copper is usually yours, while damaged drywall, flooring and cabinets may be covered depending on your policy. Ask your insurer. We provide documentation.

    Keep water pressure under about 80 psi. A pressure valve does it. Treating hard or aggressive water, insulating pipe in cold spots and limiting how long a recirculation pump runs slow down the corrosion that thins copper. Know where your main shutoff is.

    Clamps and epoxy putty can stop a drip for a day. Temporary fixes fail. A proper repair means cutting out the thin copper and soldering or pressing in new pipe, and a DIY patch that lets go while you are at work can flood a room in the time it takes to drive home. Treat it as a stopgap.

    We typically use Type L copper. It is heavier than Type M. Type L lasts longer than the Type M found in many older homes, and in some spots we use ProPress fittings or a PEX transition instead of solder. All materials are potable-rated.
