    Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Drain Cleaning Method Do You Need?

    By Aaron Mitchell · August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

    What Is Actually in the Pipe

    Before we pick a tool, we want to know what the clog is made of. A sudden stop after wipes go down is not the same job as a kitchen drain that has been getting slower for months. Roots and grease respond to different methods. The history tells us a lot.

    Crawlspaces are the most common foundation here, though basements are more common in hilly areas where homes are built into slopes.

    Ground movement opens joints, and open joints let roots in. A root mass in a sewer line is one of the most common reasons a cable machine comes back out wrapped in fibers. It also tells us a simple clearing will not last. Roots regrow fast.

    Drain Snaking: The Standard First Step

    A drain snake, also called an auger or a cable machine, is a long coiled steel cable with a cutting head on the end. We feed it into the drain, the motor spins it, and the head bores through the clog. Most calls take under an hour. Sink and tub lines get a small handheld machine with quarter-inch or 3/8 cable. On a main line we use a bigger unit with 1/2 or 3/4 inch cable and a cutter sized to the pipe.

    • Good for: hair, wipes, a single hard blockage, light roots
    • Limits: opens a channel but leaves grease and scale on the pipe walls
    • Price: usually the lower-cost option

    The weakness is that a snake opens a hole but does not clean the pipe. A 4-inch line coated in grease might get a 2-inch channel through the middle, which flows fine for a few weeks. Then the remaining grease catches more debris and the clog comes back. Repeat calls are the sign.

    Hydro Jetting: Cleaning the Whole Pipe

    A hydro jetter pushes water through a hose at 3,000 to 4,000 psi on a typical residential line. The nozzle has rear-facing jets that pull it forward up the line and scour the pipe wall all the way around as it goes. It cuts grease and sludge off the walls. Roots get sliced off flush with the joint.

    Mineral buildup from harder water can shorten the life of water heaters and leave scale on faucets, showerheads and dishwasher parts.

    Scale and grease are where jetting really earns its cost. Inside cast iron, mineral scale narrows the bore and gives grease a rough surface to grab. A descaling nozzle on the jetter brings the pipe back near its original size, so it stays clear for a year or longer, not a few weeks. Commercial kitchens jet on a schedule.

    • Good for: grease, sludge, scale, recurring roots
    • Limits: needs a camera check first on old or fragile pipe

    Choosing Between Them

    A first-time clog in a single fixture almost always gets a snake. It is quick and cheaper, and it fixes the problem most of the time. When the same line clogs again in a few months, or the cable cannot get through, we run a camera and then usually jet it. The camera decides.

    Homes built in the 1980s and early 1990s may have gray polybutylene supply lines, which have a history of failure and are a common repipe target.

    Pipe condition limits the choice. Full-pressure jetting in cracked clay or rusted-through cast iron can open the damage further and turn a cleaning into a dig. With an older line, we put a camera in first and either jet at reduced pressure or stick to a cable if the pipe is fragile. PVC and newer cast iron handle full pressure fine.

    Booking the Right Service

    Tell the dispatcher which drains are slow and how long it has been happening. Knowing that, we can usually send the right equipment the first time.
